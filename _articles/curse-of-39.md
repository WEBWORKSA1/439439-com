Most unlucky numbers come from sounds or scripture. Afghanistan's has a stranger origin: a rumour. Since at least 2011, reporters have described Afghans going to great lengths to avoid the number 39.

## What people believe

In popular belief, 39 is linked to *morda-gow*, a slang term for a pimp that literally means "dead cow". Nobody agrees on exactly how the link began, but the stigma is real enough to change behaviour. NPR's 2011 report described drivers refusing number plates that contain 39 and people avoiding phone numbers and house numbers with it.

## A committee that changed its number

The belief even reached politics. During a 2011 loya jirga, the traditional grand assembly, one committee was due to be numbered 39. Members objected, and according to reports at the time it was renumbered 41. Al Jazeera covered the same stigma in early 2012.

## How people work around it

- **Number plates:** owners ask for different plates or alter the way the number is displayed.
- **Phones:** numbers containing 39 are hard to sell and easy to refuse.
- **Addresses:** some residents avoid houses or flats numbered 39.

## The same digits, the opposite meaning

Fly east to Japan and 39 becomes one of the friendliest numbers there is. In Japanese number puns, 3 can be read *san* and 9 *kyū*, so 39 sounds like the English "thank you". The ninth of March, 3/9, is informally marketed as Thank You Day. Italy's international dialling code is +39, and nobody there thinks twice about it.

That contrast is the heart of number culture: meaning is local. ## Why it matters if you trade internationally

If you sell to Afghan customers or run campaigns in Dari or Pashto, avoid 39 in prices, phone numbers, model names and promo codes.
