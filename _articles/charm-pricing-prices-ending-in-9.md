Walk through any shop and you will see the same ending again and again: $4.99, ₹499, €19.99. The habit is called charm pricing, and it is not superstition. Researchers have tested it in the field and found it changes what people buy.

## The evidence

In a set of field experiments published in 2003, Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester varied the endings of prices in mail-order catalogues. Prices ending in 9 increased demand in all three experiments. The effect was strongest for new items, where shoppers had no reference price, and weaker when the item was already marked as on sale.

Two years later, Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz explained why. Their studies of the left-digit effect showed that people judge a price ending in 9 as meaningfully lower than one a cent higher only when the leftmost digit changes. $2.99 feels much cheaper than $3.00; $2.89 and $2.90 feel almost the same.

## Why our brains do it

We read numbers left to right and start forming a judgment before we finish. The first digit anchors the impression, so a drop in that digit carries far more weight than its actual value. A price of 439 feels "four hundred and something"; 440 is still four hundred and something, but 400 to 399 crosses a line.

## Using price endings well

1. **Use 9 where it moves the left digit.** $19.99 instead of $20 matters; $17.99 instead of $18 matters less.
2. **Use it on new or unfamiliar products,** where shoppers lack a reference price.
3. **Do not stack signals.** If an item is already labelled as on sale, the 9 adds less.
4. **Consider round prices for premium goods,** where a clean number can signal quality.
