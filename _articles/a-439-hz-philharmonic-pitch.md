When an orchestra tunes before a concert, the oboe plays an A and everyone matches it. Today that A is almost always 440 vibrations per second. For a few decades, though, London's orchestras tuned to 439.

## A century of rising pitch

There was no single standard pitch in the 1800s. Instrument makers and bands pushed tuning higher because a sharper pitch sounds brighter, and in Britain the so-called old philharmonic or high pitch reached about 452 Hz. Singers complained: higher tuning strained voices in music written for lower pitches.

## Why 439, and why at 68 °F

In 1896 London's Philharmonic Society adopted a lower "new philharmonic" pitch: A = 439 Hz, defined at a temperature of 68 °F (20 °C). The temperature mattered. Wind instruments go sharp as a hall warms up, so the society specified the pitch for a warm concert hall rather than a cold one. British piano tuners followed in 1899.

## Why the world chose 440

Tuning standards kept multiplying in different countries until, in May 1939, delegates met at Broadcasting House in London and agreed on A = 440 Hz. The new technology of radio made a single standard more urgent. One argument for 440 over 439 was practical: 440 is far easier to produce electronically, while 439 is a prime number and awkward to generate from common frequencies. The 440 standard was later adopted internationally as ISO 16.

## Hearing the difference

The gap between 439 and 440 is small, about 4 cents, a twenty-fifth of a semitone. Most listeners cannot hear it in isolation, but orchestras can, and baroque ensembles today still tune much lower, often around 415 Hz, to match historical instruments.
