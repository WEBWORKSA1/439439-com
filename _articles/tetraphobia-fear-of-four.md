Step into a lift in Seoul, Hong Kong or Taipei and you may find the buttons jump from 3 to 5, or show an F where the 4 should be. This is tetraphobia, the avoidance of the number four, and it shapes everything from apartment prices to phone numbers across much of East Asia.

## It is all in the sound

In Mandarin, four is *sì* and death is *sǐ*. The two differ only in tone, close enough that 4 picks up the meaning. Cantonese, Japanese and Korean have the same problem: Japanese *shi* and Korean *sa* both echo the word for death. In Japan, people often say *yon* for four instead, just as they prefer *kyū* over *ku* for nine, which sounds like suffering.

## Where you see it

- **Buildings.** Many skip the fourth floor, label it F, or jump from 3 to 5. Hospitals and funeral halls are especially careful. Some towers in Hong Kong skip every floor that contains a 4.
- **Phone numbers and plates.** Numbers full of 4s are cheap or hard to give away, while 8s, which sound like prosperity in Chinese, sell at a premium.
- **Gifts and prices.** Sets of four are avoided as gifts, and a price ending in 4 can quietly put customers off.
- **Combinations.** 14 can sound like "want to die" in Mandarin, and 44 doubles the problem.

## Where you don't

Tetraphobia follows language, not geography. Vietnam is the notable exception: its Sino-Vietnamese word for four has a different tone from the word for death, and people mostly use the native word *bốn*, so the fear is not common there. In the West, 4 carries no such weight; 13 plays that role instead.

## What it means for business

If your customers include Chinese, Japanese or Korean families, a few small changes can matter:

1. Check your phone number, unit number and prices for 4s, especially at the end.
2. Prefer 8 (prosperity), 6 (smoothness) and 9 (long-lasting) in Chinese-facing numbers.
3. Avoid launching on the 4th or 14th if a Chinese-speaking audience is your focus.
4. Never give a set of four as a corporate gift in these markets.
