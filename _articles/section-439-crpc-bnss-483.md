For decades, families across India learned one number in the worst week of their lives: 439. "A 439 application" meant asking a Sessions Court or High Court for bail. Since 1 July 2024 the provision has a new home and a new number, Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS). Here is what it does, in plain English.

## What Section 439 CrPC did

Section 439 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 was headed "Special powers of High Court or Court of Session regarding bail". It gave those two courts three main powers:

1. **Grant bail** to a person in custody, including for serious offences, and attach conditions.
2. **Change or cancel conditions** that a magistrate had imposed when granting bail.
3. **Order re-arrest**: direct that a person already released on bail be arrested and taken into custody.

Because these courts sit above magistrates, an accused person refused bail by a magistrate, or charged with an offence a magistrate cannot try, would usually apply under this section.

## Safeguards built into the section

Before granting bail for an offence triable only by a Court of Session, or punishable with life imprisonment, the court had to give notice of the application to the Public Prosecutor, unless it recorded in writing why notice was not practicable. Later amendments added further safeguards for certain sexual offences against women and children, including notice periods and the presence of the informant at the hearing.

## What changed on 1 July 2024

Three new criminal laws replaced the old ones on 1 July 2024: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (replacing the Indian Penal Code), the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (replacing the CrPC) and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (replacing the Evidence Act).

The bail power of High Courts and Sessions Courts now sits in **Section 483 BNSS**, with the same heading and the same structure as the old Section 439. The cross-references inside it now point to the new laws. For orientation, the commonly cited counterparts are:

| Old CrPC | New BNSS | Subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Section 437 | Section 480 | Bail in non-bailable offences |
| Section 438 | Section 482 | Anticipatory bail |
| Section 439 | Section 483 | Special bail powers of High Court and Court of Session |

Cases already pending when the new laws began are generally handled under the old Code through the BNSS's repeal and savings provision, which is why you will still see "439" in orders and petitions for some time.

## What this means in practice

- New bail applications to a Sessions Court or High Court are filed under Section 483 BNSS.
- Older orders, case law and commentary on Section 439 remain a guide, because the wording is largely carried over.
- The court still weighs the seriousness of the offence, the risk of absconding or tampering with evidence, and the person's circumstances.

## Other laws numbered 439

The number appears elsewhere in the statute books. Section 439 of India's Companies Act, 2013 makes offences under that Act non-cognizable, so courts act on a written complaint. In the United Kingdom, section 439 of the Companies Act 2006 requires quoted companies to put the directors' remuneration report to a shareholder vote.
